Disability & Medicare

Filing for Social Security Disability in Texas & the Medicare Waiting Period

By Bryant Waldron, Licensed Independent Insurance Agent · Serving Richmond, Sugar Land & Fort Bend County, TX

Medicare isn't only for people 65 and older — if you qualify for Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI), you can become eligible for Medicare at any age. But there's a well-known catch: a waiting period between approval and when Medicare coverage actually starts. Here's how filing works in Texas, and what that waiting period really looks like.

How to file for SSDI in Texas

SSDI is a federal program, so the rules are the same nationwide, but Texas applicants have the same three ways to apply as anyone else:

  • Online at ssa.gov — available 24/7, and you can save your progress as you go
  • By phone at 1-800-772-1213 (TTY 1-800-325-0778) to complete an application with an SSA representative
  • In person at your local Social Security field office — Houston, Richmond, Sugar Land, and other Fort Bend County residents are served by nearby SSA field offices

What you need to qualify

SSDI eligibility comes down to two separate tests, and you have to meet both:

  • Work credits: Most adults need 40 credits (about 10 years of work), with 20 of those credits earned in the 10 years right before your disability began. Younger workers may qualify with fewer credits.
  • Medical standard: Your condition must prevent you from performing substantial gainful activity (SGA), and it must be expected to last at least 12 months or result in death. In 2026, SSA's SGA threshold is $1,620 per month for non-blind individuals.
Texas has one of the higher initial-denial rates in the country for SSDI applications. Thorough medical documentation from every treating provider — not just a diagnosis, but records showing how the condition limits your ability to work — makes a meaningful difference in a first-time decision.

The waiting period: how long until Medicare actually starts

This is the part that surprises a lot of people. Approval doesn't mean immediate coverage — there are two separate waits stacked on top of each other:

  1. 5-month SSDI waiting period — cash benefits don't begin until 5 months after your established disability onset date.
  2. 24-month Medicare waiting period — Medicare eligibility begins 24 months after your SSDI cash entitlement starts, not 24 months after your approval letter arrives.

Put together, most people reach Medicare about 29 months after their disability onset date — not their approval date. Because of this, the onset date SSA assigns to your case (found in your award letter) matters just as much as the approval itself, since it's the actual date the clock starts running.

Two exceptions with no 24-month wait

  • ALS (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis): Medicare starts the same month SSDI entitlement begins — no 24-month wait at all.
  • ESRD (end-stage renal disease): May qualify for Medicare on a different, often much shorter timeline if you require regular dialysis or a kidney transplant, with specific rules based on your treatment situation.

What to do during the coverage gap

The 24-month gap is a real and often difficult stretch without Medicare. Common options people use to bridge it include:

  • Medicaid, if you qualify based on income and the specific rules in Texas
  • A spouse's employer-sponsored health plan, if available
  • COBRA continuation coverage from a previous employer
  • A Health Insurance Marketplace plan

Once Medicare eligibility begins

When your 24-month wait ends, Medicare enrollment is generally automatic if you're already receiving SSDI — you don't need to file a separate Medicare application in most cases. At that point, it's worth reviewing your options the same way anyone else new to Medicare would: Original Medicare with a Medigap policy, or a Medicare Advantage plan, along with whether a Special Needs Plan might apply to your specific situation.

This article explains the general federal rules and is not legal, tax, or financial advice. Individual SSDI cases vary — onset dates, retroactive benefits, and appeals can all shift these timelines. For help with your specific SSDI application or appeal, consult the Social Security Administration directly or a disability attorney.
