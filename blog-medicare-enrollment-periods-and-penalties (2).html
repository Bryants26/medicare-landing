Enrollment Periods

Medicare Enrollment Periods & Late Penalties: The Complete Guide

By Bryant Waldron, Licensed Independent Insurance Agent · Serving Richmond, Sugar Land & Fort Bend County, TX

Most of the stress around Medicare comes down to one thing: timing. Apply in the wrong window, or skip a step you didn't know applied to you, and it can mean a penalty that follows you for as long as you have that coverage. Here's every enrollment period, how to actually apply, and exactly how the penalties work.

Initial Enrollment Period (IEP)

Your first and most important window. It's 7 months long, centered on your 65th birthday: 3 months before your birthday month, your birthday month, and 3 months after.

Enrolling in the first 3 months generally gets your coverage started the month you turn 65. Enrolling later in the window can push your start date back, so earlier is better.

Special Enrollment Period (SEP)

Still working at 65 with coverage through your (or a spouse's) employer with 20+ employees? You can delay Parts A and/or B without penalty. Once that employment or coverage ends, you get an SEP: 8 months to sign up for Part B, starting the month after employment or coverage ends, whichever comes first. You do not have to wait for the General Enrollment Period.

COBRA and retiree coverage do not count as active employer coverage for this purpose — the SEP clock starts when active employment or coverage actually ends, not when COBRA runs out.

General Enrollment Period (GEP)

Missed your IEP and don't qualify for an SEP? You can enroll during GEP — January 1 through March 31 every year. Coverage starts the month after you enroll. This is the fallback window, and it's also where Part B late penalties most often get triggered, since it usually means time passed without coverage.

Annual Enrollment Period (AEP)

Once you already have Medicare, October 15 – December 7 each year is when you can switch Medicare Advantage plans, switch Part D plans, or move between Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage for the following year.

How to actually apply

Already collecting Social Security? You don't need to do anything — you're enrolled in Parts A & B automatically, and your card arrives about 3 months before your 65th birthday.

If you need to apply yourself, you have three options: online at ssa.gov/medicare/sign-up (fastest, available anytime), by phone at 1-800-772-1213 (TTY: 1-800-325-0778), or in person at your local Social Security office.

Delaying Part B because of active employer coverage? You'll submit form CMS-40B (Application for Enrollment in Part B) along with form CMS-L564 (Request for Employment Information), which your employer fills out to document your coverage. Keep a copy — it's your proof later if a penalty question ever comes up.

The penalties — what they actually cost

This is the part people are most surprised by: these aren't one-time fees. In most cases, they're added to your premium for as long as you have that coverage.

Part A Late Enrollment Penalty: Most people get Part A premium-free because they or a spouse paid Medicare taxes long enough while working. If you're one of the few who has to pay a Part A premium and don't sign up when first eligible, the penalty is a 10% increase on your premium, paid for twice the number of years you could have had Part A but didn't.

Part B Late Enrollment Penalty: This one trips up the most people. Miss your window with no SEP protection, and your Part B premium goes up 10% for each full 12-month period you were eligible but not enrolled — a penalty that generally lasts as long as you have Part B. Wait 3 years past your IEP, and that's a permanent 30% increase on your premium.

Part D Late Enrollment Penalty: Go 63 or more days in a row without Part D or other creditable drug coverage after your IEP ends, and you'll owe a penalty when you do enroll — 1% of the national base beneficiary premium for each full month you went without coverage, added to your Part D premium for as long as you have Part D. This one is easy to trigger without realizing it, especially if an employer drug plan wasn't actually creditable.

None of these penalties apply if you have qualifying coverage elsewhere during the gap — that's exactly what the Special Enrollment Period exists to protect. Keep records showing your coverage was active and creditable, in case it's ever questioned.

How to avoid all of it

  • Mark your IEP window on a calendar the moment you know it's coming
  • If delaying due to employer coverage, confirm in writing that it's active group coverage and that any drug coverage is creditable
  • The moment employer coverage ends, start your SEP clock — don't wait, and don't assume COBRA extends it
  • If you're unsure whether your situation qualifies for an SEP, ask before your window closes, not after
