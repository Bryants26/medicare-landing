Applying for Medicare: Enrollment Periods, How to Apply & Late Penalties Explained

By Bryant Waldron, Licensed Independent Medicare Insurance Agent · Richmond & Sugar Land, TX

Most of the stress around Medicare comes down to one thing: timing. Apply in the wrong window, or skip a step you didn't know applied to you, and it can mean a penalty that follows you for the rest of your life. This guide walks through every enrollment period, exactly how to apply, and exactly how the penalties work — so nothing catches you off guard.

The Enrollment Periods, All in One Place

Initial Enrollment Period (IEP)

Your first and most important window. It's 7 months long, centered on your 65th birthday:

  • 3 months before your birthday month
  • your birthday month
  • 3 months after your birthday month

Enrolling in the first 3 months generally gets your coverage started the month you turn 65. Wait until your birthday month or later, and your start date pushes back, so earlier is better within this window.

Special Enrollment Period (SEP)

If you're still working at 65 and covered by a group health plan through your (or a spouse's) employer with 20+ employees, you can delay Parts A and/or B without penalty. Once that employment or coverage ends, you get an SEP:

  • 8 months to sign up for Part B, starting the month after employment or the coverage ends (whichever comes first)
  • You do not have to wait for the General Enrollment Period

Important: COBRA and retiree coverage do not count as active employer coverage for this purpose — the SEP clock starts when the active employment or coverage actually ends, not when COBRA runs out.

General Enrollment Period (GEP)

If you missed your IEP and don't qualify for an SEP, you can enroll during GEP: January 1 – March 31 every year. Coverage now starts the month after you enroll (this rule improved a few years back — it used to mean waiting until July). This is the fallback window, and it's also where Part B late penalties most often get triggered, since it usually means time passed without coverage.

Annual Enrollment Period (AEP) — for Part C & D changes

Once you already have Medicare, October 15 – December 7 each year is when you can switch Medicare Advantage plans, switch Part D drug plans, or move between Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage for the following year.

How to Actually Apply

If you're already collecting Social Security, you don't need to do anything — you're enrolled in Parts A & B automatically, and your card arrives about 3 months before your 65th birthday.

If you need to apply yourself, you have three options:

  • Online: ssa.gov/medicare/sign-up — fastest option, available anytime
  • By phone: 1-800-772-1213 (TTY: 1-800-325-0778)
  • In person: your local Social Security office

Delaying Part B because of active employer coverage? You'll need to submit form CMS-40B (Application for Enrollment in Part B) along with form CMS-L564 (Request for Employment Information), which your employer fills out to document your coverage. Keep a copy — this is your proof later if a penalty question ever comes up.

The Penalties — What They Actually Cost

This is the part people are most surprised by: these penalties aren't one-time fees. In most cases, they're added to your premium for as long as you have that coverage.

Part A Late Enrollment Penalty

Most people get Part A premium-free because they or a spouse paid Medicare taxes long enough while working. If you're one of the few who has to pay a Part A premium and you don't sign up when first eligible, the penalty is a 10% increase on your premium, and you pay that extra amount for twice the number of years you could have had Part A but didn't.

Part B Late Enrollment Penalty

This is the one that trips up the most people. If you don't sign up when first eligible (and don't qualify for an SEP), your Part B premium goes up 10% for each full 12-month period you were eligible but not enrolled — and this penalty generally lasts as long as you have Part B. Wait 3 years past your IEP with no SEP protection, and that's a 30% permanent increase on your premium.

Part D Late Enrollment Penalty

If you go 63 or more days in a row without Part D coverage (or other "creditable" drug coverage — meaning coverage that pays at least as much as standard Medicare drug coverage) after your IEP ends, you'll owe a penalty when you do enroll. It's calculated as 1% of the "national base beneficiary premium" for each full month you went without creditable coverage, rounded to the nearest $0.10, and it's added to your Part D premium for as long as you have Part D coverage. This one is easy to trigger without realizing it — for example, if an employer drug plan wasn't actually creditable, or there was a gap between plans.

The One Silver Lining

None of these penalties apply if you have qualifying coverage elsewhere (active employer group coverage for Parts A/B, or creditable drug coverage for Part D) during the gap — that's exactly what the Special Enrollment Period exists to protect. The key is documentation: keep records showing your coverage was active and creditable, in case it's ever questioned.

How to Make Sure You Never Get a Penalty

  • Mark your 65th birthday and your 7-month IEP window on a calendar the moment you know it's coming
  • If you're delaying due to employer coverage, confirm in writing (from HR or your plan) that it's active, group coverage, and that your drug coverage is creditable
  • The moment employer coverage ends, start your SEP clock — don't wait, and don't assume COBRA extends your window
  • If you're unsure whether your situation qualifies for an SEP, ask before your window closes, not after
