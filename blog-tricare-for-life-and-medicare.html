TRICARE For Life and Medicare: How They Work Together

By Bryant Waldron, Licensed Independent Insurance Agent · Serving Richmond, Sugar Land & Fort Bend County, TX

Military retirees turning 65 face a wrinkle most civilians don't: TRICARE For Life. Here's how it coordinates with Medicare, and what you're still required to do even though you already have military health coverage.

You still have to enroll in Medicare

This is the single most important thing to know: TRICARE For Life requires you to be enrolled in both Medicare Part A and Part B to keep your TRICARE coverage active. Having TRICARE doesn't exempt you from Medicare enrollment — it works alongside it, not instead of it.

Missing your Part B enrollment window can jeopardize your TRICARE For Life coverage and lead to a lifetime Medicare late-enrollment penalty. This is one of the most common — and most costly — mistakes among military retirees approaching 65.

How the two coordinate

Once you have both Medicare and TRICARE For Life active, they work together as coordinated coverage:

  • Medicare typically pays first for Medicare-covered services
  • TRICARE For Life then pays most or all of what's left over
  • For services Medicare doesn't cover but TRICARE does, TRICARE becomes the primary payer
  • You generally don't need a Medicare Advantage or Medigap plan on top of this combination, since TRICARE For Life already fills most of the gaps Original Medicare leaves

Do you need a separate Part D drug plan?

Generally, no — TRICARE For Life includes prescription drug coverage through the TRICARE Pharmacy Program, which typically means you don't need to enroll in a separate stand-alone Part D plan. Enrolling in an unnecessary Part D plan can sometimes create coordination complications, so this is worth confirming rather than assuming either way.

What about VA benefits?

VA benefits, TRICARE, and Medicare are three separate systems that can all apply to the same person depending on your situation. Understanding which one pays for what — and when — is worth walking through individually, since it depends on your specific combination of benefits and the type of care involved.

A common point of confusion

Many veterans assume that because they've had reliable military health coverage for decades, Medicare enrollment is optional or redundant. It isn't — skipping it, even temporarily, is one of the more common and avoidable mistakes among retirees turning 65.
